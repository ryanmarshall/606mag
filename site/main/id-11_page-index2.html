  • “In the early days of the process of identification, the identity of a person was established through his signature . The invention of photography was a turning point in the history of this process. It is no less significant for criminology than the invention of the printing press is for literature.   • Next time you're thinking about streaking in the Loop or expressing your gregarious and righteous self, be aware that you have a voyeur: Big Brother is watching. The public eye is sharper than ever before with the installation of several more security cameras in the Loop area of downtown Chicago. There are a good number of cameras functioning, divided into the usage categories of government, private, traffic and public domain. At least 10 exist solely for the purpose of monitoring traffic intersections. New York artist Dennis Beaubois employs theatrics to address the growing concern of surveillance on the streets of his home city. His art interacts with the cameras through the use of posters directed towards the cameras to communicate his concerns of identity and privacy.


  • Scientists in Britain are lobbying to build the world's most powerful matterscope. Unlike its archaic microscope counterpart, a matterscope bounces around neutrons that make the waves needed to create an image of individual atoms. The neutrons hurdle down a concrete tunnel one kilometer in length to gain the necessary speed. At $1.7 billion per scope, they better see something.


  • The next time the power goes out in the middle of the night and nature calls, rest assured that all public rooms such as bathrooms are marked with Braille, as required by law for public buildings. Braille illiterate, are ya? Just don't have an accident.

  • The Talking Book Center provides the free loan of recorded and Braille books and magazines, music scores in Braille and as well as recorded materials for residents of Chicago who are unable or have difficulty reading standard print materials because of visual or physical impairment.

  • Looking to increase security or have a little spying to do? Check out Chicago Spies and find products ranging from pepper spray to hidden cameras. It's your all-in-one spy shop depot.


  • Ever wish you could look through people's clothes walking down the street? X-rays have a higher frequency wave than visible light, which is why we don't see it during our daily lives. They can penetrate through skin and tissue – but not denser objects like bones and metal – making what lies beneath visible without peeling off our skin. Conventional photographic film is used because it causes the same chemical process as visible light. Doctors only use the negatives from the film to view our bones and organs.


  • You're leaving the house and you're through the door when a little nagging voice calls out, “Did you turn the coffee pot off?” Shit! No! Umm … maybe! Rushing upstairs, you prove that you turned it off. Oh, no! Now where are your keys?

    In only a few years, the Boston-based company Micro Optical will place their newly developed memory glasses on the market. The glasses contain a miniature television that transmits prerecorded messages that flash at you throughout the day. The images zoom at your eyes at 180 th of a second, a speed not consciously detectable. But the company claims that the glasses will improve memory by 50 percent. So, where are your keys now?

  • Dust mites, whose microscopic size allows them to feed on dead skin while remaining undetectable to the human eye, thrive in pillows and sheets. If you need an incentive to do laundry this week, visualize this microscopic meal while falling asleep as a mental exercise.


  • The left and right retinas of babies are not sufficiently developed enough to work together until about four months of age. Until that time, babies, like dogs, need high contrast images to make visual sense of their surroundings. Black and white patterns are said to work best.


  • The Chicago Harbor Lighthouse, the first lighthouse built on Lake Michigan, dates back to 1832 and is still functioning, but not open to the public.

  • A German restaurant chain in Berlin and Cologne, Unsicht-Bar (achtung: this page is in German), serves meals that are concealed from patrons' view. All customers are blindfolded and forced to focus on their other senses while eating. The concept was created by the Organization of the Blind and People with Poor Eyesight. More than two-thirds of the staff is visually impaired.


  • Astigmatism is defined as an uneven curvature of the eyeball's front surface, or cornea. Eyeballs are often assumed to be round like baseballs, but many eyes are actually more akin to footballs in shape. It is this oblong shape that can cause distorted vision in astigmatic eyeballs.


  • In 1933, Ralph Wiley, a college student working as an equipment washer in the Dow Chemical Laboratory, wasn't able to scrub off debris on a piece of glassware. What he actually discovered in the debris was a Saran film. The product of a chemical reaction, Saran films were found to seal out moisture and air and eventually were used to create Saran Wrap.

Hotscotch was first developed by ancient Romans in the area that is now Great Britain. Originally the "board" was as large as a football field - or larger. The board was filled with obsticals in which training soldiers had to practice running through. The purpose of this was to help the Roman fighters form balance so that they could kill with more accuracy and strength when on foot. Local children quickly created their own modified version of the military excersize - which is similar to how it is still played today. The oldest American form of the game has 10 sections with the final goal to enter into Heaven. Today hopscotch still aids in helping people improve thier physical balance.
